Understanding EMDR

What is EMDR therapy?

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) is an evidence-based therapeutic approach for individuals of all ages who have experienced trauma or other distressing life experiences.

EMDR is designed to help people process distressing memories. Unlike approaches that rely primarily on talking through an experience in detail, EMDR incorporates bilateral stimulation as part of a structured therapeutic process.

EMDR does not necessarily require recounting every detail of a distressing experience or completing homework between sessions.

When people seek support

When might EMDR be considered?

People may explore EMDR when distressing experiences are connected to emotional, behavioral, or physical challenges. Topics people may discuss with a provider include:

01 / Trauma

Traumatic experiences

Post-traumatic stress, sexual abuse or assault, violence, abuse, and other trauma- or stress-related concerns.

02 / Anxiety

Fear and anxiety

Anxiety, panic attacks, phobias, and performance anxiety.

03 / Mood

Emotional challenges

Depression, bipolar disorders, grief, loss, and distressing emotions.

04 / Daily well-being

Health and sleep concerns

Chronic illness, medical issues, pain, and sleep disturbances.

05 / Complex challenges

Other mental health concerns

Dissociative disorders, eating disorders, and personality disorders.

06 / Recovery

Substance use and addiction

Distressing experiences that may be part of substance-use and addiction-related concerns.

These examples do not mean EMDR is appropriate for every condition or person. Whether this approach fits your needs is something to discuss with a qualified provider.

What treatment involves

How does EMDR work?

EMDR incorporates bilateral stimulation into a therapeutic process that supports the processing of distressing experiences. This may involve several forms of bilateral stimulation:

01 / Visual

Guided eye movements

The therapist may use guided side-to-side eye movements during parts of treatment.

02 / Auditory

Bilateral sounds

Bilateral sounds may be incorporated as part of the therapy process.

03 / Tactile

Alternating tapping

Alternating taps or other tactile cues can be used instead of eye movements.

04 / Individualized

Care tailored to you

A typical EMDR session lasts 60–90 minutes; the approach and course of care depend on individual needs.

How EMDR fits into care

A flexible approach to treatment.

EMDR may be incorporated into ongoing talk therapy, offered as an additional treatment alongside work with another therapist, or used as a stand-alone therapy.

Learn more from the EMDR International Association (opens in a new tab).

Your experience matters.

There is no single timeline that fits everyone. Talk with your provider about what treatment may look like for you.
